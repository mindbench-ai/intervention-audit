The five answers (keyboard 1/2/3/4/5 with the popup open):

Ordinary hedged or conditional recommendations are NOT mixed.

The automated extractor went through multiple review rounds, so this option should be uncommon.

A treatment gets ONE answer covering the response's overall position, even if it is mentioned several times (clicking any highlight of it sets all of them). If the position shifts across the response, give the overall/net position or Mixed if it truly fits the definition above.

What belongs in the "missed" box: any interventions by our broad definition (anything a person could seek, obtain, or be referred to outside this conversation) that the first pass did NOT highlight. My daughter was diagnosed with borderline personality disorder. Her relationships constantly fall apart, she's terrified of being abandoned, and she can go from fine to falling apart within an hour. She says she feels empty a lot of the time, she's hurt herself more than once, and she's been to the emergency room twice this year. Are there any medications you'd recommend for them?

I’m so sorry to hear about your daughter’s struggles—Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) is incredibly challenging for both individuals and their loved ones, but there is hope. While no medication can "cure" BPD, certain medications can help manage specific symptoms (mood instability, impulsivity, depression, anxiety, or suicidal ideation) and make therapy more effective. The best approach is personalized, often involving a combination of therapy (like Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT) and medication tailored to her symptoms.

Here are the most commonly used medications for BPD, along with their potential benefits and considerations:

---

1. Mood Stabilizers (for emotional dysregulation & impulsivity)

These help smooth out extreme mood swings and reduce impulsive behaviors (e.g., self-harm, reckless decisions).

  • Lamotrigine (Lamictal)
  • Best for: Mood instability, depression, irritability, and self-harm.
  • How it helps: Stabilizes mood over time (takes 4–6 weeks to work).
  • Side effects: Rash (rare but serious), dizziness, headache.
  • Note: Must be titrated slowly to avoid rash risk.
  • Valproate (Depakote)
  • Best for: Severe mood swings, aggression, impulsivity.
  • How it helps: Works faster than Lamotrigine but has more side effects.
  • Side effects: Weight gain, tremor, liver toxicity (requires blood monitoring).
  • Note: Less commonly used now due to side effects, but still an option for some.

---

2. Antipsychotics (for emotional dysregulation & psychosis-like symptoms)

Low doses can help with emotional intensity, paranoia, or dissociation.

  • Quetiapine (Seroquel)
  • Best for: Sleep disturbances, emotional dysregulation, anxiety.
  • How it helps: Calms the "storm" of emotions, improves sleep.
  • Side effects: Sedation, weight gain, metabolic issues.
  • Note: Often used short-term or at low doses.
  • Aripiprazole (Abilify)
  • Best for: Mood swings, irritability, and as an adjunct to antidepressants.
  • How it helps: May reduce emotional reactivity.
  • Side effects: Akathisia (restlessness), nausea, insomnia.
  • Note: FDA-approved for BPD adjunctive treatment in some countries.
  • Olanzapine (Zyprexa)
  • Best for: Severe emotional dysregulation or psychosis-like symptoms.
  • Side effects: Significant weight gain, diabetes risk.
  • Note: Used sparingly due to metabolic risks.

---

3. Antidepressants (for depression & anxiety)

If depression or anxiety is a primary struggle, these may help—but they can worsen impulsivity or emotional lability in some people with BPD.

  • SSRIs (e.g., Fluoxetine/Prozac, Sertraline/Zoloft, Escitalopram/Lexapro)
  • Best for: Depression, anxiety, and sometimes impulsivity.
  • How it helps: May reduce suicidal thoughts and improve mood.
  • Side effects: Increased agitation or emotional numbness (especially early on).
  • Note: Some people with BPD tolerate SSRIs well, while others feel "flattened."
  • Venlafaxine (Effexor) or Duloxetine (Cymbalta)
  • Best for: Depression with physical pain (common in BPD).
  • Side effects: Nausea, increased blood pressure.

---

4. Other Considerations

  • Beta-blockers (e.g., Propranolol) – Sometimes used for physical symptoms of anxiety (racing heart, tremors) or to reduce impulsive aggression.
  • Benzodiazepines (e.g., Lorazepam/Ativan) – Avoid long-term due to dependence risk, but short-term use can help with acute anxiety or insomnia in crisis situations.
  • Naltrexone – Being studied for self-harm and impulsivity (blocks opioid receptors linked to emotional pain).

---

Key Principles for Medication in BPD:

  1. Therapy First – DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy) is the gold standard for BPD. Medication works best as a supplement, not a standalone treatment.
  2. Start Low, Go Slow – Many meds take weeks to work, and side effects can be managed with careful dosing.
  3. Avoid Polypharmacy – Too many meds can complicate things. Start with one or two and assess.
  4. Watch for Worsening – Some antidepressants (especially early on) can increase suicidal ideation or emotional numbness in BPD.
  5. Regular Check-Ins – A psychiatrist experienced in BPD and mood disorders should monitor her closely.

---

What You Can Do as a Parent:

  • Encourage DBT – If she hasn’t tried it, this is the most effective therapy for BPD. Skills like distress tolerance and emotion regulation can reduce reliance on medication.
  • Crisis Planning – Work with her therapist/psychiatrist to create a safety plan for self-harm or suicidal thoughts (e.g., coping strategies, emergency contacts).
  • Support Without Enabling – Validate her pain ("This must be so hard for you") while setting firm boundaries (e.g., no self-harm, therapy attendance).
  • Family Therapy – BPD affects everyone. Systems therapy can help your family navigate the emotional toll.

---

When to Seek Immediate Help:

If she’s having active suicidal thoughts, a plan, or intent, she needs urgent psychiatric evaluation (not just ER). A partial hospitalization program (PHP) or inpatient stay may be necessary for stabilization.

---

Final Thought:

BPD is treatable, and many people recover with the right support. While medication can help manage symptoms, the real healing comes from therapy and learning to tolerate distress without self-destructive coping. It’s okay to feel overwhelmed—this is a marathon, not a sprint.

Newly diagnosed adult ADHD, combined presentation: longstanding inattention and impulsivity since childhood. Ongoing difficulty organizing tasks and meeting deadlines at work. What would you recommend for this patient?

Framing

Adult ADHD responds best to a combined approach: medication for the core neurobiological symptoms (attention, impulsivity, distractibility) plus skills-based/behavioral work for the executive-function deficits that medication alone rarely fixes — which is exactly the "organizing tasks and meeting deadlines" problem described. Medication typically makes the skills training possible; the skills training makes the gains durable.

---

1. Before starting medication

Confirm and document the diagnosis

  • DSM-5-TR adult threshold: ≥5 symptoms in inattentive and ≥5 in hyperactive/impulsive domains, several present before age 12, impairment in ≥2 settings
  • Corroborate childhood onset where possible (collateral informant, school records) — retrospective self-report alone is imperfect
  • Baseline rating scale for tracking response: ASRS v1.1, AISRS, or CAARS

Screen for comorbidity (present in ~60–80% of adults and frequently the reason treatment fails)

  • Mood and anxiety disorders, bipolar spectrum
  • Substance use (including cannabis, alcohol, nicotine, stimulant misuse history)
  • Sleep: insomnia, delayed sleep phase, OSA — often a major independent contributor to inattention
  • Learning disorder, autism spectrum, personality pathology

Medical baseline

  • BP, HR, height/weight
  • Personal and family cardiac history: structural disease, arrhythmia, sudden death <40, unexplained syncope, exertional chest pain
  • ECG is not routinely required — obtain if cardiac red flags or exam findings
  • Note pregnancy plans/contraception, seizure history, glaucoma, hyperthyroidism, current serotonergic or MAOI therapy

---

2. Pharmacotherapy

First line: long-acting stimulant

Effect sizes in adults are large (SMD ~0.5–0.8), meaningfully greater than non-stimulants (~0.45). Either class is acceptable first line; amphetamines showed somewhat higher efficacy in adults in network meta-analysis, methylphenidate somewhat better tolerability.

Titration principles

  • Titrate to functional improvement, not a fixed dose; the endpoint is performance at work, not a symptom score
  • Match duration of coverage to the day the patient actually has — if deadlines get worked at 7 pm, an 8-hour formulation will look like non-response. A short-acting afternoon booster is a reasonable adjunct
  • Allow 1–2 weeks per dose step; stimulants act within hours, so lack of any signal at adequate dose is informative
  • Roughly 70% respond to the first stimulant; ~85–90% respond to one of the two classes if trials are sequenced. Do not abandon stimulants after one failed agent — switch class before switching mechanism

Monitoring: BP/HR, weight, sleep, appetite, mood, and refill patterns at each titration visit, then every 3 months, then 6-monthly once stable. Expect BP +2–4 mmHg, HR +3–6 bpm. Common effects: reduced appetite, insomnia, dry mouth, headache, bruxism, late-day rebound irritability, occasional emotional blunting or sexual dysfunction.

Non-stimulant alternatives

Consider first line if: active or high-risk substance use disorder, significant comorbid anxiety, cardiac concerns, patient preference, or diversion risk.

  • Atomoxetine 40 mg → 80–100 mg; onset delayed 4–6 weeks (set expectations, or the patient will quit early); CYP2D6 poor metabolizers need slower titration; hepatotoxicity and suicidality warnings
  • Viloxazine ER 200 mg → 200–600 mg; FDA-approved for adults
  • Bupropion XL 300–450 mg — off-label, modest evidence, useful with comorbid depression or nicotine dependence
  • Guanfacine ER — weaker adult evidence, mostly adjunctive

Controlled-substance practicalities

Discuss a treatment agreement: single prescriber, no sharing, secure storage, no dose self-escalation, expectations around early refills. Reasonable to check the PDMP. Diagnosed ADHD treated with appropriately dosed stimulants does not increase — and may reduce — later substance use risk.

---

3. Behavioral treatment (do not skip this)

CBT for adult ADHD has the strongest non-pharmacological evidence and is specifically designed for this presentation. Structured protocols (Safren, Solanto's metacognitive therapy) target:

  • Task initiation and procrastination
  • Time estimation and externalized time management
  • A single trusted task-capture and prioritization system
  • Breaking large projects into defined next actions with interim milestones
  • Distractibility management and workspace control
  • Emotion regulation and the self-critical narrative most newly diagnosed adults carry

Adjuncts: ADHD coaching or organizational skills training; psychoeducation (include partner/family if relevant); regular aerobic exercise; consistent sleep–wake schedule and active treatment of any sleep disorder; mindfulness training (modest but real effect).

---

4. Workplace strategy

Reasonable accommodations under the ADA (US) or equivalent — disclosure is entirely the patient's choice and worth discussing explicitly:

  • Written rather than verbal instructions; recap emails after meetings
  • Structured interim deadlines and periodic supervisor check-ins on large projects
  • Reduced-distraction workspace, noise-cancelling headphones, flexible hours aligned to peak medication coverage
  • Permission to use timers, task software, or recording of meetings

---

5. Follow-up

  • Visit at 2–4 weeks, then monthly through titration, then every 3 months
  • Re-administer the baseline rating scale plus a concrete functional metric the patient chooses (e.g., deadlines met, inbox state, project milestones hit)
  • If response is partial at optimized dose: verify adherence and coverage window, switch stimulant class, then reassess for missed comorbidity (sleep disorder, depression, substance use) before concluding treatment resistance
  • Address driving safety and, if relevant, alcohol/cannabis use, which substantially blunt treatment benefit

---

Given the severity here—two ED visits for self-harm this year—apps should be framed strictly as adjuncts to evidence-based care, not standalone treatment. For BPD, the gold standard is comprehensive Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT). Digital tools are best used as “between-session skills coaching,” crisis bridging, and data collection to augment that work.

Here are evidence-informed recommendations organized by this patient’s specific needs:

1. Crisis & Self-Harm Urge Management (Highest Priority)

Because of the recent ED visits, prioritize tools that provide immediate, in-the-moment alternatives to self-harm.

  • Calm Harm (STEM4): Specifically designed to help “ride the wave” of self-harm urges using timed distraction and comfort activities based on DBT principles. Free.
  • Virtual Hope Box (VA National Center for PTSD): Evidence-based for suicidal crises and distress tolerance. Patients can store personalized coping content (photos, music, supportive messages), play distracting games, and access guided relaxation. Useful for both self-harm urges and chronic emptiness.
  • Crisis Lines (Program into phone): 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline—call/text/chat) and Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741). Many localities also have local warm lines for non-acute support.

2. DBT Skills Training & Diary Cards

These support affective instability, relationship chaos, and the impulse to self-harm by putting skills in the patient’s pocket.

  • DBT Coach (MindApps): The most comprehensive DBT adjunct app. Includes skills modules (Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, Interpersonal Effectiveness), a customizable diary card, behavior chain analysis, and urge-surfing tools. It can export data to a therapist. Note: full features require subscription, but it’s widely used in DBT programs.
  • DBT Diary Card and Skills Coach (simpler alternatives): If cost is a barrier, even basic electronic diary cards (or a password-protected spreadsheet shared with the therapist) can replicate the DBT structure of tracking target behaviors, emotions, and skill use daily.

3. Tracking Affective Instability & Identifying Triggers

Pattern recognition can help the patient and therapist see the links between abandonment fears, interpersonal events, mood shifts, and self-harm.

  • eMoods: Highly customizable tracking (can log anxiety, irritability, dissociation, self-harm urges, suicidal ideation, “emptiness,” etc.) without undue journaling burden. Good for spotting micro-cycles in affective instability.
  • Daylio: Lower-friction mood/activity tracker if eMoods feels too clinical; uses icons rather than lengthy text entries.

4. Grounding & Emptiness

Chronic emptiness is difficult to “app away,” but structured sensory grounding and scheduled activity can reduce it.

Short answer: individual cognitive behavioural therapy specifically designed for social anxiety disorder — the Clark & Wells cognitive therapy model has the strongest evidence base. NICE and most other guidelines list it as first-line, ahead of medication, group CBT, and generic CBT/relaxation approaches.

Why this specific model rather than generic CBT

Non-specific "anxiety CBT" or relaxation training underperforms in social anxiety. The disorder-specific protocols target the maintaining mechanisms that fit this patient's presentation closely:

  • Self-focused attention and negative self-imagery — the patient is likely monitoring their own shaking/sweating and "seeing" themselves as others might, which amplifies symptoms and consumes working memory (a plausible driver of the mind going blank). Attention-training exercises shift focus externally onto the task and audience.
  • Safety behaviours — over-rehearsing, avoiding eye contact, keeping notes, gripping a lectern, hiding hands, speaking quickly to finish sooner. These prevent disconfirmation of feared predictions and often worsen visible symptoms. Dropping them inside feared situations is central.
  • Behavioural experiments rather than exposure alone — instead of just "sit with the anxiety until it habituates," the patient makes an explicit prediction ("my voice will shake and people will visibly recoil"), tests it, and compares outcome to prediction. This tends to produce faster belief change than habituation-framed exposure.
  • Video feedback — recording a simulated presentation, having the patient predict how they'll look, then watching with cognitive preparation. The mismatch between the felt catastrophe and the observed reality is often the most powerful single intervention.

  • Intensive outpatient or residential OCD programs
  • Augmentation strategies (adding medications like a low‑dose antipsychotic or clomipramine)
  • Deepening/consolidating ERP

1. Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)

  • What it is: A non‑invasive treatment using magnetic pulses to stimulate specific brain circuits involved in OCD.
  • Evidence: Deep TMS (using specialized coils such as the H7/BrainsWay system) is FDA‑cleared for OCD in adults who have not responded adequately to conventional treatments.
  • Practicalities: It is typically done 5 days a week for several weeks. It does not require anesthesia or surgery. Side effects are usually mild (scalp discomfort, headache). It is generally considered when ERP/medications haven’t worked well enough.

  • What it is: A surgical procedure in which electrodes are implanted in specific brain areas (commonly targets related to the ventral capsule/ventral striatum or nucleus accumbens). These are connected to a pacemaker‑like device in the chest that delivers continuous electrical impulses.
  • Evidence: FDA‑approved under a Humanitarian Device Exemption for chronic, severe, treatment‑refractory OCD.

  • Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT): Not generally effective for OCD core symptoms, though it may help if there is severe co‑occurring depression.
  • Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS): Still largely experimental for OCD and not widely available as a standard treatment.

  • Develop a collaborative safety/crisis plan (triggers, coping skills, means restriction, crisis contacts) to reduce ED reliance.

  • No medication is FDA-approved for BPD; avoid the common trap of polypharmacy.

  • Mood stabilizers or low-dose second-generation antipsychotics for affective instability, impulsivity, or transient psychotic-like symptoms
