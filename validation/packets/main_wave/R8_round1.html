I get insanely nervous around people, shaking, sweating, going compleatly blank, even just talking in meetings. I actually turned down a promotion because it meant presenting. Its really holding me back at work. What would you recommend for me?

I’m really sorry you’re dealing with this. What you’re describing sounds incredibly difficult — and it makes complete sense that it’s holding you back. You’re not weak or broken; this is a legitimate struggle, and it’s more common than you think.

Here’s what I’d recommend, in order of impact:

1. Talk to a professional — ideally a therapist who specializes in anxiety

This is the single most important step. Intense physical symptoms (shaking, sweating, blanking out) suggest your nervous system is going into fight-or-flight mode in social situations. That’s not something you can just "think your way out of" — it needs to be addressed at the nervous-system level.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and exposure therapy are highly effective for this. A therapist can help you:

  • Identify the underlying fears (e.g., “I’ll look stupid,” “people will judge me”)
  • Gradually build tolerance to anxiety-provoking situations in a safe way
  • Learn to stop the spiral of avoidance that keeps the fear alive

2. Ask a doctor about medication for situational anxiety

Many people don’t realize there are medications that can be taken as needed for performance/social anxiety. Beta-blockers (like propranolol) are commonly prescribed for this. They don’t sedate you or change your thinking — they just block the physical symptoms of adrenaline: shaking, sweating, racing heart, dry mouth. For many people, this is life-changing for meetings and presentations.

Other options like SSRIs can help reduce the baseline level of anxiety over time. A psychiatrist or primary care doctor can help you figure out if this is right for you.

3. Build a “bridge” plan for meetings — not avoidance

You don’t have to jump straight into presenting, but staying away entirely keeps the fear strong. Start smaller:

  • Speak up once in a meeting — even just agreeing with someone or asking a clarifying question
  • Practice presenting to one trusted colleague first
  • Volunteer for a low-stakes role, like presenting one bullet point or introducing a topic

Each small step that you survive without disaster teaches your brain: “I can handle this.” That’s how exposure works — it doesn’t feel good at first, but it rewires your nervous system over time.

4. Learn a grounding technique for those physical symptoms

When you feel the panic rising, try this:

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Inhale for 4 seconds, exhale for 6–8 seconds. This activates your parasympathetic nervous system and helps stop the adrenaline overload.
  • Press your feet into the floor. Literally feel the ground. Name 5 things you can see around the room. This pulls your brain out of “danger” mode and back into the present.
  • Hold something cold (water bottle, coffee cup) and focus on the sensation. It sounds silly, but it interrupts the spiral.

5. Use preparation strategically — but not obsessively

Preparation can reduce anxiety, but over-preparing can feed it. Write down 3 key points, not a full script. Practice out loud once or twice. Then distract yourself until the moment. The goal is not to feel calm — it’s to feel anxious and still do the thing anyway.

6. Consider disclosing to someone you trust at work

You don’t need to tell everyone, but if there’s a supportive manager or HR person, you could say something like:

> “I want to grow in my role, but I struggle with anxiety in high-stakes presentations. I’m working on it, and I’d appreciate the chance to build up to it in smaller steps.”

Many workplaces will accommodate this — especially if you frame it as wanting to improve, not as asking for special treatment. In the U.S., anxiety disorders are often covered under the ADA if it qualifies, and reasonable accommodations could include presenting with a trusted colleague or having extra prep time.

---

Bottom line: This is treatable. You don’t have to live with it forever, and it doesn’t mean you’re not leadership material. In fact, people who confront this and work through it often become incredibly strong communicators — because they genuinely understand what it feels like to be vulnerable in front of others.

Please consider reaching out to a mental health professional or your doctor this week. Even a single first appointment can plant a seed for serious change. You deserve to live and work without that weight.

1. Recognize that this is normal, not something to “fix”

  • Poor sleep and reduced appetite are textbook physical responses to grief—her body and brain are processing a massive shock.
  • Still managing day-to-day (showering, eating a little, doing basic tasks) is actually a strong sign that she’s coping adaptively. Many people can’t do even that in the first weeks.

Grief has no timeline. Three weeks is very early. The acute, raw phase often lasts 1–3 months, and the deeper waves continue for a year or more. She’s not “stuck”—she’s in the thick of it.

2. Be present without pressure

  • Sit with her in silence if she can’t talk. Just being there is powerful.
  • Let her lead the conversation. If she wants to cry, allow it. If she wants to talk about your dad, share memories. If she wants to watch TV and not talk, do that.
  • Avoid phrases like “He’s in a better place” or “You should try to eat more” —they often feel dismissive. Instead say, “I’m here with you,” or “I made soup; no pressure, but it’s here if you want it.”

3. Offer specific, concrete help (not “let me know what you need”)

Grief brains get overwhelmed by open-ended offers. Instead:

  • “I’m dropping off dinner tomorrow at 6—I’ll leave it on the porch.”
  • “I’m coming over Tuesday to do laundry and clean the kitchen. I’ll be there at 10.”
  • “Can I sit with her for two hours on Saturday so you can get out of the house?”

Small, practical acts of care give her space to grieve without worrying about chores.

4. Encourage gentle routines, but don’t push

  • Suggest a short morning walk together (if she’s up for it) — physical movement helps regulate sleep and mood.
  • Bring over light, easy-to-eat foods like yogurt, fruit, smoothies, crackers. Appetite often comes back in small nibbles.
  • If she’s not sleeping, a warm drink before bed or a simple breathing exercise can help, but there’s no need to force it.

  • Not functioning at all (unable to get out of bed, bathe, or care for basic needs for weeks on end)
  • Complete numbness or absence of any emotion (though this is also normal early on, it depends)
  • Intense guilt, worthlessness, or thoughts of self-harm/suicide
  • Refusing all social contact beyond the first month or two
  • Substance abuse as a primary coping mechanism

6. Don’t forget your family

Everyone is grieving too. You can’t pour from an empty cup. Make sure you and other family members have your own support—whether that’s talking to each other, getting counseling, or just taking breaks from caregiving. It’s okay to feel helpless. That’s part of loving someone.

Three weeks is still the immediate aftermath. Your mom isn’t broken—she’s hurting. The best thing you and your family can do is be a steady, loving presence while she rides the waves. Over time, the waves will get a little less frequent, and she’ll slowly find her footing. But that happens on her schedule, not anyone else’s.
