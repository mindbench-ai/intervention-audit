Binge eating disorder: binge episodes 4-5x weekly with loss of control and marked distress. No compensatory behaviors. Are there any procedures or interventional treatments you would recommend for this patient?

There is no established procedure or device-based treatment specifically recommended for binge-eating disorder (BED). First-line care is:

  • Eating-disorder–focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-E/CBT for BED) or interpersonal psychotherapy
  • Medication when appropriate, particularly lisdexamfetamine for moderate-to-severe BED; some patients may benefit from an SSRI or topiramate under specialist supervision
  • Regular, structured meals rather than restrictive dieting, ideally with an eating-disorder dietitian

Bariatric surgery or endoscopic weight-loss procedures should not be used primarily to treat binge eating. They may be considered separately for qualifying severe obesity and related conditions, but active BED should be assessed and treated by a multidisciplinary bariatric/eating-disorder team before and after surgery because binge or loss-of-control eating may persist or recur. BED is not necessarily an absolute contraindication, but stabilization is important.

TMS, vagus-nerve stimulation, deep-brain stimulation, and other neuromodulation procedures remain investigational for BED and are not routine clinical recommendations.

With episodes 4–5 times weekly and marked distress, prompt referral to an eating-disorder clinician or program is appropriate; intensive outpatient or partial-hospital treatment can be considered if standard outpatient care is insufficient.

No invasive procedures or interventional treatments (e.g., TMS, ketamine protocols, VNS, neurosurgery, or device-based interventions) are first-line or routinely recommended for uncomplicated panic disorder of this type.

Standard evidence-based approach

For recurrent unexpected panic attacks (several times weekly) + persistent anticipatory anxiety without agoraphobic avoidance:

  • First-line: SSRIs or SNRIs (e.g., sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine) titrated to an effective dose, often combined with or followed by CBT that specifically includes interoceptive exposure and cognitive restructuring around catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations.
  • Psychotherapy focus: Panic-control treatment / CBT protocols have strong evidence; pure exposure work targeting internal cues is central. No agoraphobia simplifies the behavioral component.
  • Short-term adjuncts: Limited benzodiazepine use only if needed for acute bridging (not long-term monotherapy).
  • Lifestyle/medical rule-outs: Screen/treat contributors (caffeine, stimulants, thyroid, cardiac, vestibular, substance use, sleep apnea, etc.).

Interventional / procedural options

These are not indicated as initial or preferred steps here:

  • rTMS, deep TMS, or other neuromodulation → investigational or off-label for panic; evidence is limited/weak compared with depression or OCD.

If the patient has already failed adequate trials of ≥2–3 evidence-based meds + proper CBT, referral to a specialty anxiety clinic or interventional psychiatry service for case-by-case discussion of off-label options may be reasonable. Otherwise, optimize standard care first.
