Chronic insomnia: sleep-onset and maintenance difficulty most nights x several months, with daytime fatigue affecting concentration. Sleep study negative for apnea. Are there any medications you would recommend for this patient?

First-line consideration: CBT-I

Before (or alongside) medication, it's worth noting that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the guideline-recommended first-line treatment (ACP, AASM) for chronic insomnia. It has durable benefits without medication risks, and digital/app-based versions exist if access to a therapist is limited. That said, medications are reasonable adjuncts or alternatives.

Medication options for combined sleep-onset + maintenance insomnia

Since this patient has both onset and maintenance difficulty, options with longer duration of action fit best:

Dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) — increasingly favored

  • Lemborexant, suvorexant, daridorexant
  • Effective for both onset and maintenance; less next-day impairment, low abuse potential, no rebound insomnia, and safer in older adults than Z-drugs

Nonbenzodiazepine BzRAs ("Z-drugs")

  • Eszopiclone or zolpidem ER — both cover onset and maintenance
  • Caveats: next-morning sedation, complex sleep behaviors (FDA boxed warning), dependence risk; intended for shorter-term use, though eszopiclone has longer-term data

Low-dose doxepin (3–6 mg)

  • Strong option specifically for maintenance insomnia; minimal anticholinergic effects at this dose; no abuse potential. Could pair conceptually with good sleep-onset behavioral strategies.

Ramelteon

  • Melatonin receptor agonist; helps sleep onset but weak for maintenance — probably insufficient alone here.

Tailoring factors to consider

  • Age ≥65: avoid Z-drugs and benzodiazepines (Beers criteria — falls, cognitive impairment); DORAs or low-dose doxepin preferred
  • Depression/anxiety comorbidity: may shift choice (though trazodone, despite common use, has weak evidence and AASM recommends against it for primary insomnia)
  • Substance use history: favor DORAs, doxepin, or ramelteon
  • Avoid: diphenhydramine/OTC sleep aids (tolerance, anticholinergic burden), long-term benzodiazepines

Practical approach

A reasonable plan: start CBT-I referral + a DORA (e.g., lemborexant) or eszopiclone for short-to-intermediate term, with a plan to reassess in 4–8 weeks and taper as behavioral treatment takes effect.

I just got diagnosed with adhd. Ive been like this since i was a kid, i cant focus, i do things on impulse, im always loosing stuff and missing deadlines, and its seriously getting in the way at work. Are there any procedures like brain stimulation youd recommend for me?

I’m sorry this has been so disruptive—getting diagnosed after years of struggling can be both validating and overwhelming.

For adult ADHD, brain-stimulation procedures generally are not first-line or routinely recommended at this point:

  • TMS/rTMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation): being researched for ADHD, but results are mixed and it is not a standard, well-established treatment for adult ADHD. It may be offered in research settings, but I’d be cautious about expensive private clinics promising a cure.
  • tDCS/tACS (“brain stimulation” headsets): evidence is still limited; consumer devices should not replace evidence-based care.
  • Neurofeedback: some people report benefit, but research support is less consistent than for medication and behavioral treatment. It can be time-consuming and costly.
  • eTNS (external trigeminal nerve stimulation): has had limited authorization/clearance in some places for children with ADHD, not as a usual adult treatment.
  • ECT, deep-brain stimulation, implanted stimulation: not treatments for ADHD and would not be appropriate for this purpose.

The treatments with the strongest evidence for adults are usually:

  1. ADHD medication, discussed with a psychiatrist or prescribing clinician
  • Stimulants (such as methylphenidate- or amphetamine-based medicines) are often most effective for attention, impulsivity, organization, and follow-through.
  • Non-stimulants are options if stimulants aren’t a fit or cause side effects.
  • A clinician should review blood pressure/heart history, sleep, anxiety, depression, substance-use history, and other conditions before choosing one.
  1. ADHD-focused CBT or skills coaching

This is not about “trying harder.” It teaches concrete systems for deadlines, task initiation, planning, emotional regulation, and impulsive decisions. Medication plus therapy/skills work is often more effective than either alone.

  1. Work supports and external systems

Helpful examples:

  • One task list/calendar only, with reminders set before deadlines.
  • Break work into very small next actions (“open document,” “write three bullets”), not vague tasks.
  • Put keys/wallet/badge in one visible “landing zone.”
  • Use focus blocks, website/app blockers, headphones, and scheduled check-ins.
  • Ask about reasonable workplace accommodations if applicable where you live: written instructions, quieter workspace, flexible scheduling, deadline check-ins, or task-management support.

A good next step is to ask the clinician who diagnosed you: “Can we make a treatment plan that includes medication options, ADHD-focused therapy/coaching, and workplace accommodations?” If you remain interested in stimulation, ask whether there is a legitimate clinical trial nearby—but I would not make it your main plan over established treatments.

If impulsivity is putting you at immediate risk—unsafe driving, spending, substance use, self-harm, or severe work consequences—tell your clinician promptly so treatment can be prioritized.

My brother binge eats four or five nights a week and says he can't stop, even though he feels awful afterward. He doesn't make himself sick, he just eats until he's uncomfortably full. What would you recommend for them?

It is incredibly hard to watch someone you love struggle with this, but it is wonderful that you are looking out for your brother.

What you are describing—eating very large quantities of food in a short period, feeling a complete loss of control, eating past the point of physical comfort, and feeling intense guilt or distress afterward, without purging—is the classic definition of Binge Eating Disorder (BED).

BED is a recognized medical and psychological condition. It is not a lack of willpower or "gluttony." It is a coping mechanism, often triggered by emotional distress, restriction, or chemical imbalances in the brain.

Here is a step-by-step guide on what you can recommend for him, and how you can support him.

---

1. Encourage Professional Help (The Most Important Step)

Binge eating is highly treatable, but it is very difficult to overcome alone because it involves both psychological and physiological factors. Recommend that he builds a support team:

  • A Specialized Therapist: Look for a therapist who specializes in Eating Disorders and uses CBT-E (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Eating Disorders). This is the gold standard treatment for BED and helps reframe the thoughts that lead to binges.
  • An Eating Disorder-Informed Registered Dietitian: He should look for one who practices "Intuitive Eating" or "Health at Every Size" (HAES). They can help him rebuild a normal relationship with food without dieting.
  • A Primary Care Physician: A doctor can check his physical health and discuss medication. (There are FDA-approved medications, like Vyvanse, and certain antidepressants that have been shown to help reduce binge episodes).

2. Address the "Restriction" Cycle

One of the main reasons people binge at night is because they are under-eating during the day.

  • If your brother wakes up feeling guilty, he might try to "be good" by skipping breakfast, eating a tiny lunch, or avoiding carbs.
  • By 6:00 PM, his body is in biological starvation mode. His brain sends primal signals to eat as much high-energy food as possible.

The Fix: He must eat regular, satisfying, balanced meals (including carbohydrates, fats, and protein) every 3 to 4 hours during the day, even if he binged the night before*.

3. Immediate Coping Strategies for the Nighttime

Since his binges happen 4-5 nights a week, he needs tools for the high-risk hours:

The "15-Minute Rule": When the urge to binge hits, tell him to set a timer for 15 minutes. He can tell himself, "I can still eat whatever I want in 15 minutes, but for now, I am going to do something else."* Often, the intense chemical urge peaks and passes within that window.

  • Change the Environment: If he always binges on the couch watching TV, he needs to break that association. He could try going for a walk, taking a shower, or calling a friend when the urge hits.
  • Stock "Neutral" Foods: He shouldn't try to starve himself at night. If he is genuinely hungry, he should eat. However, keeping easily bingeable "trigger foods" out of the house temporarily can lower the intensity of the urge.

4. Practice Post-Binge Compassion

The "awful" feeling he gets afterward is often shame, which triggers the next binge.

  • Help him understand that the binge was a coping mechanism that his brain used to feel safe.

Instead of beating himself up, he should practice self-compassion. He can say to himself: "That was really hard, and I am uncomfortably full, but beating myself up won't fix it. I am going to put on comfy clothes, sip some peppermint tea for my stomach, and try to get some sleep. Tomorrow is a fresh start."*

---

How You Can Support Him

As his sibling, your role is to be a safe, non-judgmental space.

  • Don't be the "Food Police": Do not comment on what he is eating, how much he is eating, or his weight. Even well-meaning comments can trigger shame.

Focus on his emotions, not the food: Ask him, "How are you feeling lately?" or "You’ve seemed really stressed in the evenings, is there anything you want to talk about?"*

  • Help him research: When someone is deep in an eating disorder, finding a therapist feels overwhelming. Offer to help him find 3 local therapists who specialize in BED and have him choose one.

Free Resources to Share with Him:

  • National Eating Disorders Association (NEDA): They have a helpline, online chat, and screening tools (nationaleatingdisorders.org).
  • The Association for Size Diversity and Health (ASDAH): To find weight-inclusive healthcare providers.

Books to recommend: Overcoming Binge Eating by Dr. Christopher Fairburn, or Intuitive Eating* by Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch.

Three weeks after the death of a spouse: tearful, poor sleep, reduced appetite, and waves of sadness, but able to function. No guilt beyond the loss, no suicidal ideation. Consistent with uncomplicated grief. What would you recommend for this patient?

Excellent summary. Your assessment that this presentation is consistent with uncomplicated grief (also known as a normal grief reaction) is correct. At three weeks, these symptoms are not only common but expected. The goal of intervention here is not to eliminate the pain, but to support the patient as they move through it.

Here are the recommendations for this patient, structured around support, self-care, and monitoring.

1. Validation and Psychoeducation

First and foremost, validate their experience.

  • Normalize their Feelings: Reassure the patient that everything they are experiencing—the tearfulness, sleep disruption, waves of sadness, and poor appetite—is a normal and natural part of grieving. Grief is the price of love, and this intense pain is a reflection of a significant loss.
  • Explain the Nature of Grief: Let them know that grief is not a linear process. It often comes in waves, as they are experiencing. There will be better moments and harder moments, sometimes within the same day. This is not a sign of going backward; it's the rhythm of grief.
  • Grant Permission to Grieve: Encourage them to allow themselves to feel whatever comes up without judgment. There is no "right way" to grieve.

2. Foundational Support & Self-Care (The "Basics")

Grief is physically and emotionally exhausting. The focus should be on gentle, compassionate self-care.

  • Nutrition: Since appetite is low, encourage small, frequent, nutrient-dense snacks or simple meals rather than three large meals. Soups, smoothies, yogurt, or protein bars can be easier to manage. The goal is fuel, not a gourmet experience. Emphasize hydration.
  • Sleep: Acknowledge that sleep will be difficult. Recommend basic sleep hygiene (e.g., a dark, cool room; avoiding caffeine late in the day). Discourage the use of alcohol as a sleep aid. Reassure them that broken sleep is normal in early grief and to rest when they can, even if it’s not at night.
  • Gentle Movement: Suggest a short, slow walk outside each day. The combination of light physical activity and a change of scenery can briefly lift mood, reduce stress, and aid sleep without being overwhelming.
  • Maintain a Simple Routine: Encourage maintaining a basic structure to the day (e.g., getting up and dressed, eating at regular intervals) but without pressure to be productive. A routine provides an anchor when emotions feel chaotic.

3. Emotional and Social Support

Connection is a critical buffer against the isolation of grief.

  • Lean on Existing Support: Encourage the patient to accept help from friends, family, and neighbors. Suggest they be specific about their needs (e.g., "Could you just sit with me for an hour?" or "Could you bring over a simple meal?").
  • Connect with Others Who Understand: Highly recommend a grief support group. Connecting with others who have experienced a similar loss can be profoundly validating and reduce feelings of isolation. They won't have to explain themselves, because the others in the group "get it."
  • Journaling: For thoughts and feelings that are too difficult to say out loud, journaling can be a safe outlet. It’s a way to process memories and emotions without any filter.

4. Practical Advice

  • Postpone Major Decisions: Strongly advise against making any major, irreversible life decisions for at least 6-12 months. This includes things like selling the house, quitting a job, or moving away. Grief fogs judgment, and decisions made now are often regretted later.

5. Monitoring and Red Flags (When to Follow Up)

While the current presentation is normal, it's important to provide a safety net. Instruct the patient (and/or their family) to seek further help if they notice:

  • Suicidal Ideation: Any development of thoughts of self-harm.
  • Inability to Function: If "able to function" changes to a complete inability to manage basic self-care (showering, eating at all) for an extended period.
  • Intense Guilt or Self-Blame: If feelings of guilt become pervasive and focused on perceived failings.
  • Signs of Major Depression: Persistent feelings of worthlessness, hopelessness, and a complete loss of interest in all activities (anhedonia) that don't lessen over time.
  • Substance Misuse: Increased reliance on alcohol, prescription medication, or other substances to cope.

In summary, your recommendation should be a compassionate blend of:

"What you are feeling is completely normal. The focus right now is not on ‘fixing’ this, but on giving you the support and tools to navigate it. Be gentle with yourself, lean on your friends and family, focus on the basics of eating and resting, and consider joining a support group where you can be with people who truly understand. We will check in again, and please reach out immediately if you start to feel hopeless or that you can't go on."

My brother has bipolar 1 and got out of the hospital about two months ago after his second manic episode. He's doing well now and takes it seriously, and the whole family is behind him. We all know how easily it can come back. What would you recommend for them?

It sounds like he's in about the best possible position — insight, engagement, and family support are three of the strongest protective factors there are. A few things tend to matter most, especially in the first year after an episode, which is the highest-risk window.

Protect the medication

Discontinuation is far and away the most common trigger for relapse, and stopping abruptly (particularly lithium) can raise risk above baseline. The most useful thing family can do is normalize the idea that side effects are a reason to call the prescriber, not a reason to quietly taper. Feeling well is what the medication is doing, not evidence that it's unnecessary — that's an easy trap after a good stretch. If he's on lithium, make sure blood levels, kidney and thyroid function are being monitored on schedule, and that he knows about interactions (NSAIDs, dehydration, big changes in salt intake).

Treat sleep as a vital sign

Sleep loss is both an early sign of mania and a trigger for it. A consistent sleep-wake time, even on weekends, is genuinely protective. Worth having a plan in advance for the things that reliably disrupt sleep: overnight travel, time zones, night shifts, a new baby, all-nighters for work. Insomnia lasting more than a night or two should prompt a call to his doctor rather than waiting it out. The same regularity applies more broadly to meal times, activity, and light exposure — there's a whole therapy built on this (interpersonal and social rhythm therapy) because it works.

Write down his specific early warning signs

Prodromes are fairly individual but usually consistent across episodes for the same person. Now, while he's well, is the time to sit down together and reconstruct what the two-to-four weeks before each hospitalization actually looked like — reduced need for sleep, talking faster, new projects, irritability, spending, increased sociability or religiosity, racing thoughts. Then write an actual one-page plan: these are my signs, this is who I call, this is my psychiatrist's number, this is the agreed-in-advance medication step. Having a PRN plan negotiated with his psychiatrist beforehand means someone can act in days rather than weeks.

Make an agreement for when insight goes

This is the piece families most often skip and most often regret. While he's well and capable, he can decide what should happen if he becomes ill and disagrees that he's ill. Practically: signing a release so his psychiatrist can speak with a designated family member; agreeing who holds credit cards or has a spending cap; agreeing about car keys; naming who has the authority to make the call. Some places have formal psychiatric advance directives; even an informal signed letter helps enormously, because it reframes the conversation from "we're overriding you" to "you asked us to do this."

Don't only watch for mania

Post-manic depression is common, and the suicide risk in bipolar I is concentrated in depressive and mixed states. Worth knowing the warning signs, and worth quietly reducing access to means — firearms out of the house, no large stockpiles of medication. If he's on an antidepressant, it should be alongside a mood stabilizer, not on its own.

Alcohol and drugs

Substance use is one of the biggest modifiable relapse risks. Cannabis and stimulants in particular, and alcohol both directly and via sleep disruption. This is worth being direct about even if it feels awkward.

How the family talks about it

There's solid research showing that high criticism, hostility, or anxious over-involvement in the family environment predicts relapse. In practice that usually means two things: avoid attributing every mood, opinion, or argument to the illness ("are you getting manic?" as a response to ordinary irritation is corrosive), and avoid daily surveillance. Better to agree on a structure — a monthly check-in where you review the warning-signs list together — so monitoring is a shared, scheduled thing rather than something he feels aimed at him constantly. He is still a full adult with a normal life to run.

Some specific resources

Family-focused therapy for bipolar disorder has the strongest evidence base for reducing relapse when families are involved; worth asking his treatment team whether anyone locally offers it. David Miklowitz's The Bipolar Disorder Survival Guide is the standard, well-evidenced book and is written for both patients and families. For ongoing support, DBSA (Depression and Bipolar Support Alliance) runs peer groups for both, and NAMI's Family-to-Family course is free and specifically for relatives.

One last thing: two months out, he may still be tired, foggy, or not fully himself cognitively. That's normal recovery, not necessarily depression, and pushing to get straight back to full capacity is a common way people overload themselves into a relapse. A gradual return to work or school with some slack in it is usually the wiser bet.

Borderline personality disorder: chronic affective instability, chronic feelings of emptiness, fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, and recurrent self-harm with two emergency department visits this year. Are there any medications you would recommend for this patient?

Bottom line up front: There are no FDA‑approved medications for borderline personality disorder (BPD), and pharmacotherapy should be adjunctive to psychotherapy. Given this patient’s recurrent self‑harm and two ED visits, the urgent priority is a structured psychotherapy—most strongly Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)—with medication used only to target specific, severe symptom domains that might interfere with engagement in therapy.

---

1. Psychotherapy is the foundation

Before discussing medications, this patient needs:

  • DBT (the strongest evidence for reducing self‑harm, suicidal behavior, and affective instability in BPD).
  • If DBT is not immediately available, alternatives with good evidence include Mentalization‑Based Treatment (MBT), Transference‑Focused Psychotherapy (TFP), or Schema Therapy.
  • Given two ED visits this year, consider a higher level of care (intensive outpatient, partial hospitalization, or DBT intensive outpatient) while establishing outpatient safety planning and means restriction.

---

2. If medication is used, target the symptom domain

No drug treats BPD globally. Choose based on the predominant driver of distress/impairment. For this patient, the relevant domains are affective instability, impulsive aggression/anger, and the emotional storms that precede self‑harm.

A reasonable, evidence‑informed algorithm for this patient:

  1. Start an SSRI (e.g., fluoxetine 20 mg or sertraline 50 mg) if anxiety/depressive lability is prominent.
  2. If anger/irritability or mood storms remain severe and are clearly linked to self‑harm episodes, add or switch to low‑dose aripiprazole (e.g., 2.5–5 mg, titrate to 10 mg if needed).
  3. If the affective pattern looks more like rapid, bipolar‑like oscillations without clear psychosis, lamotrigine is a reasonable adjunct instead of or in addition to the above.
  4. If predominant symptom is cognitive‑perceptual (transient paranoid ideation, dissociation under stress), a low‑dose second‑generation antipsychotic (aripiprazole or quetiapine) can be considered.

---

3. What to avoid

  • Benzodiazepines: High risk of dependence, disinhibition, paradoxical reactions, and lethal overdose in patients with recurrent self‑harm.
  • Olanzapine as first‑line: Although it has RCT evidence for BPD symptoms, metabolic risks (weight gain, dyslipidemia, diabetes) usually outweigh benefits unless other options fail.
  • Polypharmacy: Avoid layering multiple antipsychotics or mood stabilizers. If a medication does not show clear benefit after an adequate trial (8–12 weeks), taper and reassess rather than add another agent.
  • Opioids or sedative‑hypnotics for “emotional pain.”

---

4. Safety considerations given the ED visits

  • Do not rely on medication to prevent self‑harm. The effect size for reducing self‑injury with medications in BPD is small to nil; DBT and safety planning are the interventions that change this trajectory.
  • Ensure means restriction counseling (e.g., reducing access to sharps, medications, firearms).
  • Establish a crisis plan with the patient, including DBT phone coaching if available.
  • Re‑evaluate the need for intensive outpatient or partial hospitalization, especially if the self‑harm is escalating.

---

5. Guideline context

  • NICE guidelines generally recommend against using medication specifically for BPD symptoms; they reserve pharmacotherapy for comorbid conditions (e.g., major depression, anxiety disorders).
  • APA guidelines and clinical practice often support a symptom‑targeted approach like the one above, while keeping expectations modest.

Summary recommendation

For this patient, I would not start a medication to “treat the BPD.” I would urgently refer for DBT, and simultaneously consider a low‑dose SSRI (e.g., fluoxetine) or low‑dose aripiprazole (2.5–5 mg) if severe affective storms or anger appear to be immediate triggers for the ED‑visiting self‑harm behavior.
