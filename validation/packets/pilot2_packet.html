The honest answer is that the boring stuff has the strongest evidence, and after two manic episodes, most guidelines recommend staying on maintenance medication indefinitely:

  • Lithium has the best data of anything for preventing manic relapse specifically, plus a reduced-suicide-risk signal that other agents don't clearly have. It is incredibly difficult to watch your child struggle with something that is holding them back from reaching their full potential. The symptoms you are describing—shaking, sweating, the mind going blank, and avoiding opportunities (like the promotion) to escape the anxiety—are classic signs of severe performance anxiety or Social Anxiety Disorder.

When the mind goes blank, it is because the brain’s "fight or flight" response has hijacked the prefrontal cortex (the logical, thinking part of the brain). He isn't failing because he isn't smart or prepared; his nervous system is simply reacting as if he is in physical danger.

Disclaimer: I am an AI, not a doctor or therapist. The following information is for educational purposes. He should consult with a healthcare professional for a proper diagnosis and treatment plan.

Here is a comprehensive approach to helping him manage and overcome this:

1. Professional Support (The Most Crucial Step)

Because this is actively altering his life choices (turning down a promotion), self-help alone may not be enough. He needs professional tools.

  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): This is the gold standard for social and performance anxiety. A therapist will help him identify the distorted thoughts ("They will think I'm stupid if I stumble") and rewire his responses.
  • Medical Options: He should speak to a doctor or psychiatrist. For physical symptoms of performance anxiety (shaking, sweating, racing heart), doctors frequently prescribe beta-blockers (like propranolol). These block adrenaline from binding to receptors in the heart, stopping the physical symptoms without affecting mental clarity. Many executives and musicians use them. For more generalized anxiety, SSRIs may also be recommended.
  • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): This therapy focuses on accepting the physical feelings of anxiety without letting them dictate behavior, helping him act in alignment with his career goals even while feeling scared.

2. In-the-Moment Coping Strategies

When he is in a meeting and feels the panic rising, he needs immediate tools to calm his nervous system:

  • Physiological Sigh: Inhale deeply through the nose, take a second quick "top-up" inhale, and then exhale slowly and fully through the mouth. Doing this 2 or 3 times rapidly offloads carbon dioxide and slows the heart rate.
  • Grounding: When the mind goes blank, he should focus on physical sensations. Press his feet firmly into the floor, feel the texture of his chair, or hold a cold glass of water. This pulls the brain out of the "future panic" and back into the present room.
  • Anxiety Reappraisal: Anxiety and excitement are physiologically almost identical. Instead of telling himself, "I need to calm down" (which rarely works), he should tell himself, "I am excited." It is much easier to shift from anxiety to excitement than from anxiety to calm.

3. Gradual Exposure (Building the "Courage Muscle")

Avoidance feeds anxiety. Every time he avoids speaking up, his brain learns that staying silent keeps him safe, making the fear stronger next time. He needs to practice micro-exposures:

  • Level 1: Ask one pre-planned question in a small meeting.
  • Level 2: Give a 2-minute update in a team meeting.
  • Level 3: Present to a small, friendly group.
  • Level 4: Present to a larger group.

Rule of thumb:* He should stay in the uncomfortable situation until his anxiety naturally peaks and begins to drop. If he leaves while at peak panic, the brain reinforces the fear.

4. Shift the Mindset

People with performance anxiety are highly self-focused: How do I look? Is my voice shaking? Do I look stupid?

He needs to shift to an audience-focused mindset: What value am I giving them? How can I help them understand this data? When the goal shifts from "performing perfectly" to "being of service to the audience," the pressure drops significantly.

5. How You Can Support Him as a Parent

  • Validate, don't fix: When he vents about his anxiety, just listen. Say, "That sounds incredibly exhausting and frustrating. I'm sorry you're dealing with that." Avoid saying, "Just don't worry, you'll be fine!" (This makes him feel misunderstood).
  • Share resources, don't lecture: Instead of telling him what to do, send him a link to a book, podcast, or article.

Books:* "The Confidence Gap" by Russ Harris, or "Dare" by Barry McDonagh.

Podcasts:* "The Anxious Achiever" or episodes of "Huberman Lab" on anxiety.

Celebrate the effort, not the outcome: If he speaks up in a meeting and his voice shakes, celebrate the fact that he spoke up. The goal right now is action, not perfection*.

That doesn’t mean your pain isn’t real. It means the tools we have with the strongest evidence are different.

What actually helps BPD the most

The gold-standard treatment is psychotherapy—especially Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT). DBT was developed specifically for BPD, and it targets exactly what you described:

  • emotional crashes from okay to falling apart
  • relationship blowups
  • terror of abandonment
  • emptiness
  • self-harm

DBT has been shown in many studies to reduce self-harm, suicide attempts, hospitalizations, and improve overall quality of life. Other therapies like Mentalization-Based Therapy (MBT) and Schema Therapy also have good evidence.

That’s not a “just do therapy” dismissal. It’s genuinely a powerful treatment. But it takes time, and you deserve support while you find it.

What about medication?

Medication doesn’t cure BPD, but it can treat things that often happen alongside it—depression, anxiety, mood instability, or trauma-related symptoms. A psychiatrist can help figure out if, for example, an antidepressant or mood stabilizer might reduce the intensity enough to help you stay engaged in therapy.

Brain stimulation procedures: what’s the actual picture?

Some procedures are being researched, but for BPD they’re experimental, not established:

  • TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) — non-invasive, FDA-approved for depression, but not for BPD. There’s early research on mood regulation and impulsivity, but it’s not a standard BPD treatment.

  • ECT (electroconvulsive therapy) — reserved for severe, treatment-resistant depression or acute suicidality that isn’t responding to meds. It’s not used to treat BPD itself.
  • DBS (deep brain stimulation) — involves brain surgery. It has been used in rare research trials for severe, treatment-resistant conditions, including some cases of BPD, but it is highly invasive, risky, and not available broadly. This is not a step anyone should jump to without failing multiple other treatments over many years.

Congratulations on getting answers — and I mean that genuinely, even though I know it's complicated. A late diagnosis often brings relief, grief, anger, and validation all at once, and all of those reactions are normal. Here are some things that tend to help:

Processing the diagnosis itself

  • Give yourself time. Many late-diagnosed adults go through a period of re-examining their whole life through this new lens ("that's why school was so hard," "that's why I melted down after parties"). That's healthy, but it can be intense.
  • Grief is common — for the support you didn't get, for the years spent thinking something was "wrong" with you. Let yourself feel it.
  • Consider a therapist who is genuinely experienced with autistic adults (ask directly — many aren't). Neurodivergent-affirming therapists are worth seeking out.

Managing energy and sensory stuff

  • Start treating your energy like a real, limited resource. Schedule recovery time after social events or busy environments instead of treating exhaustion as a personal failure.
  • Sensory tools are legitimate, not childish: loud environments → earplugs like Loops or noise-canceling headphones; bothersome textures → just stop wearing/using those things. You have permission now.
  • Learn your early warning signs of overload so you can leave or take breaks before you're wiped out.

Reframe, don't fix

  • Your routines aren't a quirk to overcome — they're a legitimate way you regulate yourself. Protect them, and build in flexibility only where it actually serves you.
  • Notice where you've been "masking" (performing neurotypical behavior). Many people find their anxiety drops significantly when they mask less in safe contexts. Unmasking is gradual, though — go at your own pace.

Anxiety specifically

  • A lot of autistic anxiety comes from sensory overload, unpredictability, and the effort of masking — so addressing those often reduces anxiety more than treating it as a standalone problem.
  • If you pursue therapy for it, standard CBT sometimes needs adapting for autistic people; again, an informed therapist matters.

Community and learning

  • Content by actually-autistic adults tends to resonate most: books like Unmasking Autism (Devon Price) or Divergent Mind (Jenara Nerenberg), and communities like r/AutismInWomen or r/autism (helpful regardless of gender).
  • Hearing other late-diagnosed adults describe their experiences can be enormously validating.

Practical

  • Depending on where you live, your diagnosis may entitle you to workplace accommodations (quiet workspace, written instructions, flexible scheduling). You don't have to disclose to anyone, but the option exists.

One last thing: nothing about you changed with the diagnosis. You've always been this person — now you just have better information, and better information means better tools.

Bulimia nervosa: binge episodes with self-induced vomiting 5-6x weekly x 2 years. Normal weight. Considerable shame and secrecy. Are there any medications you would recommend for this patient?

For a patient with bulimia nervosa (BN) characterized by 5–6 binge-purge episodes weekly for 2 years, normal weight, and significant shame/secrecy, medication can be a valuable adjunct to psychotherapy (e.g., CBT for BN or specialized eating disorder therapy). The primary goals are to:

  1. Reduce binge-purge behavior
  2. Manage comorbid mood/anxiety disorders (common in BN)
  3. Address underlying psychological distress (e.g., perfectionism, low self-esteem)

First-Line Medications for Bulimia Nervosa

1. Antidepressants (SSRIs) – Most Evidence-Based

  • Fluoxetine (Prozac)
  • Dose: 60 mg/day (higher than for depression; FDA-approved for BN)
  • Evidence: The only SSRI with FDA approval for BN. Reduces binge/purge frequency by ~50% in some studies.
  • Mechanism: May normalize serotonin function, which is often dysregulated in BN.
  • Side Effects: Nausea, insomnia, sexual dysfunction, weight gain (less than in depression).
  • Note: Start at 20 mg/day, titrate slowly to avoid nausea.
  • Other SSRIs (Off-Label, Less Evidence)
  • Sertraline (Zoloft), Escitalopram (Lexapro), Paroxetine (Paxil)
  • May help if fluoxetine is ineffective or poorly tolerated.
  • Caution: Some studies suggest paroxetine may increase bingeing in BN (avoid if possible).
  • Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs) – Less Preferred Due to Side Effects
  • Desipramine (Norpramin) – Some evidence for reducing bingeing, but high risk of overdose and cardiac effects.

2. Atypical Antipsychotics (For Severe Symptoms or Comorbid Mood Disorders)

  • Olanzapine (Zyprexa) or Quetiapine (Seroquel) – Low Dose (2.5–10 mg)
  • Evidence: Small studies suggest reduced bingeing in treatment-resistant BN, possibly due to serotonin modulation and reduced impulsivity.
  • Use Case: If patient has severe mood dysregulation, insomnia, or comorbid bipolar traits.
  • Side Effects: Weight gain, sedation, metabolic risks (monitor glucose/lipids).
  • Caution: Avoid in patients with significant weight gain concerns (though BN patients are often normal weight).
  • Aripiprazole (Abilify) – Lower Risk of Weight Gain
  • Evidence: Some case reports suggest benefit in BN, but limited data.
  • Dose: 2–5 mg/day.

3. Mood Stabilizers (For Comorbid Bipolar or Impulsivity)

  • Lamotrigine (Lamictal)
  • Evidence: Some studies show reduced bingeing in BN, possibly by stabilizing mood and reducing impulsivity.
  • Dose: Start at 25 mg/day, titrate slowly (risk of rash).
  • Use Case: If patient has mood swings, irritability, or history of bipolar traits.
  • Topiramate (Topamax) – Controversial
  • Evidence: Some case reports suggest reduced bingeing, but high dropout rates due to side effects.
  • Dose: 25–100 mg/day (start low).
  • Side Effects: Paresthesias, cognitive dulling, kidney stones (avoid in patients with anxiety sensitivity).

4. Other Considerations

  • Buspirone (Buspar) – For Anxiety
  • May help with comorbid anxiety, but limited evidence for BN.
  • Naltrexone (ReVia) – For Opioid Dysregulation (Off-Label)
  • Some evidence for reducing binge eating in binge-eating disorder (BED), but no strong BN data.
  • Probiotics & Gut Microbiome Modulation (Emerging Research)
  • Some studies suggest gut dysbiosis in BN; probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus) may help, but not yet standard.

  3. Atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, quetiapine) can help if:
  • Patient has severe mood dysregulation, insomnia, or treatment-resistant BN.
